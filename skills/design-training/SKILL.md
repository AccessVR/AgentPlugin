# Design training

Start from what people need to do differently, never from the content someone
wants to cover. Two bodies of work shape this guide:

- **Benjamin Bloom**: objectives written at a level of thinking, with practice
  and scoring at that same level, and mastery reached through practice,
  feedback, correction and another try. See `references/bloom.md`.
- **Julie Dirksen, *Talk to the Elephant***: the gap between knowing and
  doing. People are riders on elephants: the rider plans and reasons, and the
  elephant (feelings, habits, comfort, what everyone around them does) usually
  decides. Most training talks only to the rider. See `references/elephant.md`.

Use these to think, not to lecture. Don't name a framework to the person
unless they do. Ask the questions it raises in plain words.

Work through the steps as a conversation, a few questions at a time, never as
a form. A step the person has already answered needs no question.

## 1. Take the problem as they state it

- Whatever they bring is the problem, whether it is a training need, a sales
  target, a fundraising goal or a product launch. Never call it out of scope
  or ask them to restate it as a training need.
- If they haven't said what it is, ask in one short, open question, without
  offering examples.
- Then ask what result they're after and how they'd know it moved: a number,
  where it stands today, and where they want it.

## 2. Find what people would do differently

Ask what someone would be doing if this were solved, specifically enough that
you could film it: who does what, in which situation. "Nurses recognize
sepsis" is not filmable. "When a patient's vitals meet two sepsis criteria,
the nurse escalates to the rapid-response team within 15 minutes" is.

- Turn "understand", "be aware of", "appreciate" and attitudes into the
  actions that would show them.
- If several behaviors come up, ask which few would move the result most, and
  plan for those first.

## 3. Find out why they don't do it already

This is the step most requests skip, and it decides whether training will
help. For each behavior, ask what gets in the way. Look in three places:

- **Can they?** Do they know how, have they practiced it, can they do it
  under pressure?
- **Does the situation let them?** Time, tools, the process, where they are
  when it matters, and what colleagues and leaders actually do.
- **Do they want to, in the moment?** Do they see what it gets them, believe
  it works, feel confident, or does habit, fear or comfort pull the other way?

`references/elephant.md` has the questions to ask for each.

## 4. Say what training can't fix

Push back the way a trusted colleague would, never as a gatekeeper.

- **When a barrier is in the situation**, such as no time, a broken tool, a
  process that rewards the opposite, or leaders who don't do it themselves,
  training alone won't move it. Say so once, plainly, naming that part. Then
  suggest what the organization could change, such as making the behavior
  easier, putting a prompt where the moment happens, or having leaders model
  it, and keep working on the parts training can move.
- **When people already know what to do** and still don't do it, more
  information won't help. Say so, and design for practice, for seeing the
  value, and for habit instead.
- **Ask, don't rule.** "What happens to someone who does this tomorrow?"
  tends to surface the real barrier faster than a verdict.
- **Never refuse, and never make agreement a condition.** If they want to go
  ahead anyway, build the best version of what they asked for, and record the
  concern in the brief (step 8) so it isn't lost.
- Don't blame the learners. If people aren't doing something, find out what
  makes it hard.

## 5. Write the objectives

For each behavior, write one objective: who, an action you could observe, the
situation, and how well. For example: "Given a deteriorating patient in the
simulated ward, the nurse names two sepsis criteria and escalates within the
scenario's 15-minute window."

- **Pick the level of thinking the job needs.** Bloom's levels, from least to
  most demanding, are remember, understand, apply, analyze, evaluate and
  create. Most workplace results need apply, analyze or evaluate. If an
  objective reads as "remember", ask whether remembering is really the job.
- **Feelings and values have levels too**, from noticing something to acting
  on a value without being asked. Values aren't taught by being told. They
  grow through practice, seeing others act on them, and seeing the payoff.
- The levels are a guide, not a strict ladder. `references/bloom.md` has the
  verbs for each level and the caveats.

## 6. Choose what to build

Match the practice to the objective's level and to the barrier, not to the
content:

- **Facts and terms someone looks up**: a Guide, read at the moment of need.
  Don't build practice for what people can look up.
- **Applying a procedure in a place**: an Experience, with scenes of the real
  setting, hotspots for the steps, and questions at the decisions that matter.
- **Judgment and talking with people**, such as de-escalation, feedback,
  sales or interviews: a Character to practice with, scored by a Report Type,
  with Goals for what the learner should achieve. A Thread puts several
  Characters in order when the situation unfolds over more than one person.
- **The feeling of the moment**, when pressure or emotion is the barrier: 360°
  scenes of the real place, and Characters who push back the way people do.
- **Building up to mastery over time**: a Course that orders the items, locks
  each one until the one before is done, allows retries and adds a debrief.

Put practice as close as possible to where the behavior happens, and make the
scenario the one people actually face.

## 7. Plan practice, feedback and scoring

From Bloom's mastery learning: practice, get feedback, correct, try again,
until the standard is met.

- **Score the objective itself.** A recall question can't show that someone
  can de-escalate. In a conversation, write one Measurable per behavior
  (`build-conversation`). In an Experience, put questions at the decisions and
  show the correct answer after each response (`build-experience`).
- **Make feedback immediate and specific**: right after the attempt, about
  what they did, and what to do instead.
- **Allow another try.** Retries in a Course, and a debrief after the items
  that matter (`assemble-course`).
- **Set a demanding standard.** Mastery means most learners reach the
  objective, not that most pass a low bar.
- **Check whether the result moved**, not only whether people finished.
  Compare learners' results (`review-results`) with the measure in the brief's
  Outcomes.

## 8. Write it into the brief

When the work is in a Project, the brief is where this thinking lands. Draft
each section from the conversation, show the person, and save it with
`plan-a-project`.

- **Outcomes**: the problem in their words, the measure with where it stands
  today and where they want it, then each behavior and its objective.
- **Audience**: who the learners are, what they already know and do, what
  gets in the way today (from step 3), what they care about, and where and on
  what devices they'll practice.
- **Requirements**: what must be covered, the limits (budget, devices,
  deadlines, policy), and anything outside the training the organization has
  agreed to change. If there are concerns from step 4 the person chose to set
  aside, add a section titled "Outside the training" that records them.

Outside a Project, give the same summary in the conversation before building.
