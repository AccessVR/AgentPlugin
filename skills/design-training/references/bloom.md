# Bloom: levels of thinking and mastery

Benjamin Bloom led the group that published the *Taxonomy of Educational
Objectives* in 1956. It was built to help write objectives and the tests that
check them so the two agree. Lorin Anderson and David Krathwohl revised it in
2001. The revision uses verbs for the levels, puts create at the top, and adds
the kinds of knowledge.

## Levels of thinking

| Level | The learner can… | Verbs for objectives | What practice and scoring look like |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Remember | recall facts and steps | name, list, identify, recall | A question with one right answer. Often better as a Guide to look up. |
| Understand | explain what something means | explain, summarize, classify, compare | Explaining back, sorting examples. |
| Apply | carry out a procedure in a real situation | perform, carry out, use, execute | An Experience set in the real place, with questions at each decision. |
| Analyze | work out what is going on | distinguish, diagnose, prioritize, find the cause | A case with signals to notice and noise to ignore. |
| Evaluate | judge and decide, and defend the call | decide, judge, recommend, justify, critique | A conversation where the learner has to choose and explain why. |
| Create | put something new together | plan, design, compose, propose | Building a plan or a pitch, scored against criteria. |

Write each objective with one verb from the level the job needs. Score it at
that same level. A learner who passes a remember-level quiz has shown nothing
about an evaluate-level objective.

## Kinds of knowledge

The 2001 revision crosses each level with the kind of knowledge involved:

- **Factual**: terms and details.
- **Conceptual**: categories, principles and how things fit together.
- **Procedural**: how to do something, and when to use which method.
- **Metacognitive**: knowing your own thinking, such as noticing when you're
  unsure and checking.

"Apply procedural knowledge" (carry out the steps) and "evaluate conceptual
knowledge" (decide which principle governs this case) need different
practice. Name both when it helps choose what to build.

## Feelings and values

Krathwohl, Bloom and Masia published the levels for feelings and values in
1964. Each level is a step deeper into holding the value:

1. **Receiving**: notices it, and is willing to listen.
2. **Responding**: takes part when asked.
3. **Valuing**: acts on it without being asked, and is seen to.
4. **Organizing**: fits it in with the other things they value, and settles
   conflicts between them.
5. **Characterization**: acts on it consistently, even under pressure.

Most "attitude" requests, such as "staff should care about safety", are asking
for levels 3 to 5. Telling people it matters reaches level 1 at best. Practice
that lets them act on the value and see the result is how they get further.
`elephant.md` covers how.

## Mastery learning

Bloom's mastery learning (1968) treats a learner who hasn't mastered something
yet as a problem with the instruction, not with the learner:

1. Teach a unit with a clear objective.
2. Check with a short practice test that counts for feedback, not the grade.
3. Give the learners who haven't reached the standard corrective help: a
   different explanation, example or practice, not the same material again.
4. Check again, then move on.

In OrchestrateXR this is a Course that locks each item until the one before
is done, allows retries, and adds a debrief after the items that matter, with
items scored against the objective.

## What the evidence supports, and what it doesn't

Treat this framework as a well-tested starting point, not as settled science.

- **The levels are a guide, not a strict ladder.** The authors of the
  revision say the categories overlap, and studies support the ordering at
  the lower levels better than at the top. Use the levels to match objectives,
  practice and scoring, not to rank people.
- **Bloom's "2 sigma" result (1984)**, where tutored students using mastery
  methods outscored a conventional class by two standard deviations, comes
  from small studies and hasn't been reproduced at that size. Later reviews
  find mastery learning helps, often by a much smaller amount, and most on
  the skills it practiced directly.
- **It works best** with a demanding standard (about 90% or higher rather
  than 70–80%), with corrective help that is new rather than repeated, with
  objectives and checks that match, and with continued practice afterward.
  The learners who start furthest behind gain the most.
- **It doesn't reach the elephant.** A learner can master the knowledge and
  the steps and still not do them at work. That is what `elephant.md` is for.
