# Talking to the elephant

Julie Dirksen's *Talk to the Elephant: Design Learning for Behavior Change*
(2023) starts from the gap between knowing and doing. It borrows Jonathan
Haidt's picture of a rider on an elephant. The rider plans and reasons. The
elephant (feelings, habits, comfort, what the people around it do) is bigger,
and when the two disagree it usually wins. Training that only explains things
talks to the rider.

## Find the behavior first

Ask "what would I be filming?" Attitudes and beliefs aren't specific enough to
design for. A behavior is someone doing something you could watch. If the
person can only describe a feeling ("they should be more confident"), ask
what a confident person would be doing that the others aren't.

## Why they don't do it: three places to look

Dirksen uses the COM-B model from behavior-change research: a behavior needs
**capability**, **opportunity** and **motivation**. Ask the person about each,
in plain words. You'll often need to ask them to find out from the learners
themselves.

**Capability: can they?**

- Do they know what to do, and when?
- Have they ever practiced it, or only heard about it?
- Can they do it under the real pressure, time limit or emotion?
- Are they physically able to, in that place, with that equipment?

**Opportunity: does the situation let them?**

- Do they have the time, the tools and the information at that moment?
- Does the process make it easy, or does it reward doing something else?
- Is the moment it matters somewhere training can reach, or in a rush, at
  night, alone?
- What do experienced colleagues and leaders actually do? If they don't do
  it, new people won't either.

**Motivation: do they want to, in the moment?**

- Do they see what doing it gets them, or only what they "must" do?
- Do they believe it works, and that they can do it?
- Does habit pull them the old way? Does it feel risky, awkward or slow?
- Does it fit who they think they are at work, and what they value?

## What training can and can't move

Training can move capability, and some motivation: confidence, seeing the
value, and practice until it feels normal.

Training alone can't fix opportunity:

- no time, a broken tool, or a process that works against the behavior
- incentives that reward the opposite
- leaders who don't do it themselves
- the moment happening where no reminder reaches

For those, name the part, and suggest the organization's lever:

- **Make it easier.** Making the right thing easier nearly always beats
  persuasion. Putting hand rub where care happens does more for hand hygiene
  than telling people it matters.
- **Prompt at the moment**: a checklist, a reminder in the tool, a sign where
  it happens.
- **Change the norm**: leaders and respected peers doing it visibly.
- **Remove the competing priority**: training on item 30 of 37 doesn't happen.

Then build training for the parts it can move, and record the rest in the
brief.

## Ways to reach the elephant

- **Make it feel real.** A 360° scene of their own ward, warehouse or sales
  floor, and a Character who pushes back the way people do, reach the feeling
  of the moment in a way that slides don't.
- **Practice the moment, not the topic.** Build the scenario they actually
  face, as close as possible to when and where they face it.
- **Show the payoff.** Explain what the behavior protects or gets them,
  rather than what they "must" or "have to" do. Acknowledge what they already
  know.
- **Plan the trigger.** "If X happens, then I do Y" plans hold up better than
  intentions. A debrief can end with the learner writing theirs.
- **Support motivation, don't try to supply it.** People can't be motivated
  by someone else, but feeling in control, getting better at something, and
  belonging all help. Let learners choose, show them their progress, and show
  peers doing it.
- **Connect to identity and values.** Show how the behavior fits values they
  already hold ("this is what good nurses do"), not new ones you ask them to
  adopt.
- **Change is a process, not an event.** Plan for practice and reminders
  after the training, not only the training itself.
- **Keep it light.** Dense content takes effort learners may not spend. Cut
  what they can look up.

## Design responsibly

Behavior change techniques work on people. Use them for changes the learners
would want if they understood them, and be open about what the training is
for.
